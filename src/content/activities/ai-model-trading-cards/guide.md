## Overview

AI Model Trading Cards builds on the unplugged example for CSTA standard MS-ALG-ML-08, "Evaluate the
features and limitations of a machine learning model." The original idea is for students to design
trading cards for two or more AI tools, listing each model's name, version, uses, training data,
limitations and ethical considerations.

This version adds three things to make it work for middle school and without devices:

- **Six fictional AI tools**, each with a one-page fact sheet modeled on a real model card. Because
  the tools are made up, every class has the same evidence, and nobody needs to look up anything
  online. Each fact sheet ends with an eight-test log from an "independent tester". Every log
  hides a weakness the maker didn't mention, so students analyze evidence instead of copying it.
- **Stats with evidence.** Students rate Reliability, Fairness, Privacy and Eco from 1 to 5 using
  the stat guide on page 2, and must give a reason for each rating. Reliability comes from
  counting results in the test log.
- **A game to use the cards.** In Right Tool for the Job, teams match their cards to real-world
  missions and argue for their choice, including when the best choice is no AI at all.

## The six tools

| Tool | Type | What students should find |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ChatPal 3 | Chatbot | Invents facts and sources, gets exact math wrong |
| PixelDream 2 | Image maker | Stereotypes, trouble with hands and words, copies living artists |
| LeafLens 1.5 | Classifier | Fails at night and on plants from outside its training regions; low confidence predicts errors |
| VoiceNote 4 | Speech to text | The "97% accurate" claim only holds for voices like its training data |
| TuneScout 6 | Recommender | Filter bubbles, ignores small artists, collects a lot of data |
| FaceKey 2 | Face unlock | Worse for darker skin in dim light and for kids; a twin can unlock it; very private |

The tools are designed to disagree: FaceKey is the most private and least energy-hungry tool but has
fairness problems, while ChatPal is useful but unreliable on facts. No tool wins every stat.

## Preparing

- Give each pair or group two fact sheets, so the class covers all six tools. Two groups with the
  same tool is fine, and makes a good comparison later.
- Print the card template on card stock if you can. Cut the mission cards (page 10) ahead of time,
  and give each team one **No AI** card.
- Decide who scores the game: the class by vote, or you.

## Running the activity

**Session 1 (45 minutes): investigate and rate**

1. **Hook (5 minutes).** Ask: "If you were choosing an AI tool for your school, what would you want
   to know about it first?" Collect answers, then show page 1 and introduce model cards.
2. **Model it (10 minutes).** Walk through the SpellRight example on page 1. Point out that the
   weakness names a test, and that "watch out" is about people, not only errors: names from some
   cultures get "fixed" more often.
3. **Investigate (25 minutes).** Groups mark each test log, answer "What do the ✗ tests have in
   common?" and fill in the Rate it box. Circulate and ask for evidence: "Which test tells you
   that?"
4. **Check in (5 minutes).** Ask each group for one hidden weakness. Note which ones connect back to
   the training data.

**Session 2 (45 minutes): make cards and play**

1. **Make the cards (20 minutes).** Students fill in a card for each tool, then use the card check
   on page 9.
2. **Right Tool for the Job (20 minutes).** Play six to eight missions. Encourage challenges, and
   make sure **No AI** wins at least once.
3. **Debrief (5 minutes).** Use the questions below.

For a single 60-minute period, give each group one tool to investigate and one to compare, and play
five missions.

## Things to watch for

- **Copying the fact sheet.** The weakness on the card should come from the test log, not the
  "Not made for" line. Ask: "Did the maker tell you that, or did you find it?"
- **Reliability isn't everything.** Students often give the best card to the tool with the most ✓s.
  Push them to consider who the ✗s happen to (Fairness) and what happens to their data (Privacy).
- **"Not tested" is an answer.** ChatPal's log doesn't compare different users, so its Fairness is
  unknown. That's a real problem with many published model cards.
- **Test 7 on PixelDream.** It did what was asked, so it's ✓, but it copied an artist who never
  agreed. Use it to separate "works" from "is OK to use".
- **Stereotypes.** Treat PixelDream tests 2 and 3 as being about the training pictures, not about
  doctors or nurses. Ask: "What pictures did it probably learn from?"

## Debrief

- Which hidden weakness could cause the most harm? Who would it harm?
- Why might a company leave a weakness off its fact sheet?
- VoiceNote says it's 97% accurate. When is that true, and when isn't it?
- How did the training data explain the weaknesses you found?
- What would you want every AI fact sheet to include?

## Going further

- **Real-world cards.** Have students make a card for an AI tool they use, from information you
  print out: a published model card, or the tool's help and privacy pages. Anything the maker
  doesn't say goes on the card as "not shared".
- **Patch notes debate.** Collect every group's patch notes for one tool. Which fix would help the
  most people? Which would cost the most?
- **Design a test.** Have students write two new tests for a tool that could reveal a weakness
  nobody has checked yet, like FaceKey with sunglasses or ChatPal with a question in another
  language.
- [The CSTA PK–12 Computer Science Standards](https://csteachers.org/pk12standards/) (MS-ALG-ML-08)
  include this idea and a computer-based version where students test a real classifier.
