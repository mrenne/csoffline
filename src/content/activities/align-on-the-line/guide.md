## Overview

Align on the Line is a thinking routine for setting AI use norms with students instead of for them.
Students brainstorm the ways AI could be used on a specific assignment, place each use on a
five-point scale from "Totally fine" to "Crosses a line", compare their maps, and agree on a class
line. People draw these lines differently depending on their values, their learning goals and the
rules they've been given, so the disagreements are the point: they make the gray areas visible.

The routine works best at the start of a unit or alongside a real assignment, and it's worth
repeating as students' skills grow. No devices are needed: students reason about AI uses without
using AI.

## Preparing

- Choose the assignment. Use one of your own, or one of the two sample sets: a persuasive essay
  (page 2) or a coding project (page 3). Each set has 12 uses that run from clearly fine to clearly
  over the line.
- For your own assignment, have students brainstorm uses on sticky notes or the blank cards
  (page 4), then pick 8–12 that cover the whole range. You can choose them, or the class can.
- Print page 1 for each student, a set of cards for each pair, and page 6 for the class poster.
  Page 6 can be enlarged or copied onto chart paper.
- Decide in advance how the class line will be set: by vote, by consensus, or by you after
  discussion. Tell students which one before you start.

## Running the activity

1. **Brainstorm** (5–10 minutes). Frame uses as what someone *could* do, not what they should do.
   Accept everything.
2. **Map on your own** (5 minutes). Students write each use, or its card code, in a column on
   page 1. Quiet, individual work first means everyone has a view before discussion starts.
3. **Compare in pairs** (5–10 minutes). Pairs lay the cards out together and talk through the uses
   they placed differently.
4. **Align as a class** (10–15 minutes). Take the uses with the most disagreement first. Sort the
   cards on the poster and move the dashed line to where the class decides it goes.
5. **Document.** Post the finished map and refer back to it when the assignment is under way.

## Variations

- **Stand on the line.** This replaces the original's live polling. Tape a line across the floor
  and post the five signs along it. Read a use aloud and have everyone stand where they'd put it.
  The spread of the class is the poll result. Ask people at each end to explain.
- **Roles.** For each use, name the role AI plays: explainer, tutor, brainstorm partner, fact
  finder, editor, coach, co-writer or ghostwriter. Then decide which roles fit this assignment.
  Page 5 has space for this.
- **Skills.** Before mapping, students rate their own skill at each part of the task: excel,
  developing or beginning. A use that's fine for someone who has mastered a skill can get in the
  way for someone still learning it.

## Tips

- Be clear about who has the final say. If you change a decision the class made, explain why.
- Remind students that the line moves: different classes, teachers and assignments can draw it in
  different places, and that's not a contradiction.
- Some uses depend on details. "Ask AI for facts" is very different from "Ask AI for facts, then
  check them yourself." Push students to say what would move a use up or down the line.
- Expect the middle column to fill up. "Not really sure" is an honest answer and a good place to
  start the class discussion.

## Debrief

- Which uses did almost everyone agree on? Which ones split the class?
- What reasons came up most: learning, honesty, fairness, accuracy, or the rules?
- Did anyone change their mind? What changed it?
- What should you do when you're about to use AI and you're not sure where the line is?
