## Overview

Paper Helicopters turns NASA JPL's Make a Paper Mars Helicopter project into a data investigation.
Students build a paper helicopter, change one thing about its design, and drop it many times to
find out what the change does. The engineering is the hook; the data is the point. The same
helicopter never falls exactly the same way twice, so students have to deal with the problem every
data scientist faces: how do you find a real pattern in results that vary?

It works across grades by changing what students do with the data:

- **Grades 3–5:** collect repeated measurements, describe the spread, and talk about why results
  vary.
- **Grades 6–8:** summarize the data with a median and range, graph it, and write up the
  investigation, including its limitations.
- **Grades 9–12:** pool the class data, compare graph types, and judge how far the evidence
  supports a conclusion.

## Preparing

- Print the template from NASA JPL, one per student plus spares. A group testing blade length needs
  at least two helicopters: one with full blades and one with shortened blades.
- Pick a drop spot that's the same for everyone, such as a marked height on the wall or the top of
  a stairwell. A higher drop gives bigger differences between designs.
- Decide what the class will measure: race results, fall time, or spins. Fall time gives the best
  data for graphing; races work best for younger students without stopwatches.
- Draw a class data table on the board, with a row for each group and a column for each drop.

## Running the activity

1. **Build and explore (10 minutes).** Students cut out and fold their helicopters, then drop
   them. Ask what they notice: which way do the blades spin? Does it fall straight down?
2. **Predict (5 minutes).** Ask: "Will shorter blades make the helicopter fall faster or slower?"
   Students write a prediction and a reason before testing.
3. **Plan a fair test (5 minutes).** Groups agree on what stays the same (drop height, how they
   let go, who times it) and what changes (only blade length).
4. **Test (15 minutes).** Each group drops each helicopter at least five times and records every
   result, including ones that look wrong.
5. **Summarize and graph (15 minutes).** Groups find the median and range for each design, make a
   dot plot, and add their medians to the class table.
6. **Discuss (10 minutes).** Use the debrief questions below.

## Making sense of the data

A dot plot is the best first graph: one dot per drop, with a separate row for each design. It shows
both the typical result and the spread, and it makes overlap easy to see.

| Drop | Full blades (seconds) | Short blades (seconds) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 2.1 | 1.6 |
| 2 | 2.4 | 1.5 |
| 3 | 1.9 | 1.8 |
| 4 | 2.3 | 1.4 |
| 5 | 2.2 | 1.6 |

In this sample, the median is 2.2 seconds for full blades and 1.6 seconds for short blades. The
ranges (1.9–2.4 and 1.4–1.8) don't overlap, so the difference is probably real. When ranges
overlap a lot, more drops are needed before anyone can say which design is slower.

Pooling the class data is where the investigation gets interesting. Groups often disagree, and the
reasons make a good list of limitations: different droppers, different reaction times on the
stopwatch, bent blades, a draft from a door, or a group that dropped from a different height.

## Things to watch for

- **One drop is not data.** Students want to drop once and decide. Point to the variation in their
  own five drops before any comparisons.
- **Throwing out results.** A drop that hit the wall or was timed late is fair to redo, but
  students should record why. Throwing out results just because they don't fit the prediction is
  how biased conclusions happen.
- **Changing two things at once.** If a group shortens the blades and adds a paper clip, they
  can't tell which change did what. Ask them which variable they're testing.
- **"Best" isn't a measurement.** Slowest fall, most spins and straightest path can pick
  different winners. Have groups say which one they mean.

## Debrief

- Why did the same helicopter give different results on different drops?
- How many drops did you need before you trusted your result?
- Did every group find the same pattern? If not, what might explain the difference?
- What could go wrong in our investigation that would make the conclusion wrong?
- Mars has only about 1% as much air as Earth. What would a helicopter need to fly there? NASA's
  Ingenuity used blades 1.2 meters across, spinning far faster than a helicopter's on Earth.

## Going further

- **Predict from a pattern.** Following the NASA project's later steps, count spins from several
  heights, graph spins against height, and use the graph to predict the spins from a new height.
  Then test the prediction.
- **Reverse it.** Have students find the single change that makes the blades spin the other way,
  and check whether it changes the fall time.
- **Compare graphs.** Older students can show the same class data as a dot plot, a bar chart of
  medians, and a box plot, then discuss what each graph hides.
- [Make a Paper Mars Helicopter](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/edu/resources/project/make-a-paper-mars-helicopter-2/)
  on NASA JPL Education has the full build instructions, a video tutorial, and a lesson plan for
  teachers.
