## What you'll do

NASA flew the first helicopter on Mars, and its engineers tested many designs before they found one
that worked. You'll do the same with paper. Build a helicopter, then find out how changing its design
changes the way it falls.

This activity is based on
[Make a Paper Mars Helicopter](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/edu/resources/project/make-a-paper-mars-helicopter-2/)
from NASA JPL Education.

## How it works

1. **Build.** Cut out the template and fold it into a helicopter. Drop it from as high as you can
   reach and watch what happens.
2. **Predict.** Will shorter blades make the helicopter fall faster or slower? Write down your
   prediction and why you think so.
3. **Plan a fair test.** Change only one thing, the blade length. Write down exactly how you'll drop
   each helicopter: from what height, how you'll let go, and what you'll measure.
4. **Test and repeat.** Drop each helicopter at least five times and record every result. One drop
   isn't enough, because the same helicopter doesn't fall exactly the same way every time.
5. **Find the pattern.** Graph your results. Compare your team's data with the rest of the class.
   Was your prediction right?

## Ways to measure

- **Race:** drop two helicopters side by side and record which one lands first.
- **Time:** time each fall with a stopwatch.
- **Spins:** tape a straightened ribbon to the bottom and count the twists after it lands.

## Afterward

- Why did the same helicopter give different results on different drops?
- What does "best" mean for a helicopter? Does a different meaning pick a different winner?
- If you changed two things at once, could you tell which one made the difference?
- Mars has only about 1% as much air as Earth. What would a helicopter need to fly there?
