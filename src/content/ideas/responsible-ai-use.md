## What's really going on

AI tools can explain ideas, give feedback, fix mistakes, or do whole tasks for you. Whether that's
a good idea depends on what you're trying to do. If the point of an assignment is to learn a
skill, letting AI do that part means you miss the learning, even if the result looks good. If the
point is something else, the same help might be fine. Using AI responsibly means thinking about
why you're doing a task, being honest about how you used AI, and checking what it gives you,
because AI can be wrong.

## Background

There is no single rule for when AI use is acceptable. People draw the line in different places
depending on their values (honesty, fairness, effort), their learning goals, and the policies of
their school, class or workplace. Researchers and educators often frame this as **agency**:
deciding deliberately what role AI should play, such as tutor, editor or co-writer, instead of
letting the tool decide. Norms work best when they are discussed openly and agreed on, because
students can then apply the reasoning to situations a rule didn't anticipate. Good norms also
cover **transparency** (saying how AI was used) and **verification** (checking AI output, which can
include made-up facts or sources).

## Misconceptions

- Using AI is either cheating or it isn't. Most uses fall somewhere in between, and where they fall
  depends on the task and its purpose.
- If AI gets the answer right, it doesn't matter who did the work. When the goal is learning a
  skill, doing the work is the point.
- Everyone agrees on where the line is. Teachers, schools and workplaces set different rules, and
  thoughtful people disagree.
- AI output can be trusted as long as it sounds confident. AI tools can state wrong information,
  including invented facts and sources, very convincingly.
