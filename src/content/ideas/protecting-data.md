## What's really going on

Schools, hospitals, stores and apps all keep information about people. Some of it is fine for
anyone to see, like a team schedule. Some of it, like health records, card numbers or a home
address, could cause real harm if the wrong person saw it. Protecting data means figuring out how
sensitive it is, deciding who really needs it, and using defenses to keep everyone else out:
passwords and two-step logins, rules about who can see what, encryption, and plain old locks and
shredders. No single defense stops every kind of attack, so good security uses several.

## Background

Security is often described with the **CIA triad**: **confidentiality** (only the right people can
see the data), **integrity** (nobody can change it without permission) and **availability** (the
right people can get to it when they need it). Organizations usually **classify** data into levels,
such as public, internal and restricted, and protect each level differently. Laws set some of the
rules: in the United States, FERPA covers student records, HIPAA covers health records, and the
payment card industry has its own security standard.

Common defenses fall into a few groups. **Authentication** proves who someone is (passwords,
two-step login). **Access control** decides what each person can see, ideally following the
**principle of least privilege**: only what their job needs. **Encryption** protects data stored on
devices and sent over networks. **Physical safeguards** protect paper and devices. **Data
minimization**, collecting and keeping less, protects data by not having it at all. Every defense
has a trade-off in cost, time or convenience, which is why security is a set of choices rather than
a checklist.

## Misconceptions

- Security is about hackers and computers. Many leaks involve paper, lost devices, open links, or a
  person who was tricked or curious.
- More security is always better. Every defense costs time, money or convenience, and a defense
  that doesn't match the threat does nothing.
- A backup keeps data safe. A backup protects availability: it doesn't keep data secret, and it's
  one more copy to protect.
- If you have a password, your data is protected. A weak or shared password, or one stolen by a fake
  email, gives an attacker the same access you have.
- Data that's already collected has to be protected forever. Often the best defense is to stop
  collecting it, or to delete it when it's no longer needed.
