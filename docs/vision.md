# Vision

## What Computation Quests is

A free, openly licensed collection of **unplugged** computer science, data science, computational
thinking, cybersecurity, and AI literacy activities for grades 6–12. Every activity works with no
technology: students use paper, cards, string, dice, the classroom floor, and each other.

It is presented as one map, a continent divided into regions, each covering one subject area.
Each region gives teachers:

- unplugged activities they can teach tomorrow, with a teacher guide and print-ready handouts
- a short primer on the topic, common misconceptions, and standards alignment
- a "connect to computing" bridge in every activity, so students see how the idea works inside
  real computers

## Why unplugged

- **Any classroom can use it.** No devices, logins, software, Wi-Fi, or tech support. It works in
  a math room, a library, a gym, or outdoors.
- **Concepts before tools.** Students learn how sorting, encryption, or machine learning works
  without fighting a programming language or an app.
- **Students are the computer.** When students carry out an algorithm, become the data, or act as
  the network, the idea becomes physical and memorable.
- **Teachers new to CS can teach it.** The materials are familiar: paper, cards, and discussion.
- **Nothing to collect.** With no devices there are no accounts and no student data.

### What "no technology" means here

- **Students use no devices** during any activity.
- **Teachers may use a computer and printer to prepare**, for example to print handouts from the
  site. Every activity also lists a no-printer option where one is practical (copy it on the
  board, or draw it by hand).
- **Projection is optional.** No activity depends on slides or a screen.

### What unplugged can and cannot cover

Unplugged activities teach the concepts behind almost every topic on the map. Some standards ask
students to write and run programs on a computer, or to use digital tools. Activities name the
standards they support, and region pages note where a standard also needs a computer, so a
teacher can pair the activity with plugged work if they want to.

| Cybersecurity | An island off the west coast |

Computational Thinking is the starting point because it underlies everything else. Ethics and
Impacts sits at the center, on every route between regions, because it belongs in every subject.

The map lives on the website, where teachers browse it. It can also become a physical object in
the classroom: a printable poster of the map, and a paper quest log where students mark the places
they have visited. (See the [roadmap](roadmap.md).)

## Design principle: human-in-the-loop (unstated)

This is a design note for authors, not a message for the site or the map. Nothing in the branding
should say it outright.

As students move through Computation Quests, they should come to realize on their own that
computing depends on people at every step: people frame the problem, choose and label the data,
write and test the code, check the output, and decide what to do with it.

Unplugged activities make this especially visible, because the students themselves carry out the
computing. They follow an algorithm exactly and see where it needed a judgment it could not make.
They sort the training cards and see their own choices in the "model." They play the network and
see who decides what gets through. For example:

- an algorithm that students run by hand until it reaches a case it cannot decide
- a paper "AI" that looks right until students check its output
- a deck of data cards whose meaning depends on choices someone made when collecting it
- a cipher or lock that is only as strong as the person using it

Students should discover this through what they do, not read it in a headline. Ethics and Impacts
is where they reflect on it explicitly.

## What makes a good activity here

1. **No student devices**, and materials a school already has or can print.
2. **Students do the computing** with their hands, bodies, and talk, usually in pairs or groups.
3. **One class period** (30–50 minutes), with under 15 minutes of teacher prep.
4. **Print-ready handouts** that copy well in black and white on letter or A4 paper.
5. **A "connect to computing" debrief** that links what students did to real computers and
   real-world systems.
6. **Accessible to everyone.** Any activity that uses movement offers a seated or tabletop version.

## Audiences

| Audience | What they need |
|---|---|
| CS teachers (MS/HS) | Standards-aligned activities, depth, and a break from screens |
| Teachers new to CS | A short content primer, clear steps, common misconceptions, familiar materials |
| Cross-curricular teachers (math, science, social studies, ELA) | Short activities that bring computational thinking or data into their subject |
| Schools with few devices | Complete CS learning that needs no technology at all |

Students meet the activities in class, through their teacher. They do not use the website.

## Principles

1. **Free.** Nothing is paywalled.
2. **Unplugged.** No activity requires students to use technology.
3. **No student data.** Students need no accounts, and the site collects nothing about anyone.
4. **Consistent structure.** Every region follows the [area template](area-template.md).
5. **Classroom-tested.** Materials come from real classroom use and improve through feedback.
6. **Open.** CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 lets teachers adapt and share the materials.
